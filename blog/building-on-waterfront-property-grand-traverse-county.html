Building on Waterfront Property in Grand Traverse County: Setbacks, Permits & What to Know

November 11, 2026 · 12 min read · By Grand Traverse Builders

Owning or buying a lot on Grand Traverse Bay, Long Lake, Silver Lake, Boardman Lake, or a river corridor near Traverse City is different from building on an inland upland parcel. The house still needs township zoning and a building permit, but shoreline work, wetlands, soil erosion control, flood mapping, and well/septic isolation can sit on the critical path months before foundation work. This guide explains which Michigan EGLE authorities commonly apply, how local townships regulate waterfront setbacks and vegetation, what to check for high water and FEMA flood zones, and how to sequence permits with your builder—with links to official sources and to our deeper building permits and well and septic guides.

Why Waterfront Builds Are Different Near Traverse City

A waterfront project usually has three parallel regulatory tracks:

  • State and federal land/water interface permits through Michigan EGLE (and often the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers) for wetlands, inland lakes and streams, and Great Lakes bottomlands.
  • County Part 91 soil erosion review for earth changes near lakes and streams, plus health-department well and septic approvals on unsewered lots.
  • Township zoning and building rules that set waterfront setbacks, greenbelts/vegetation strips, and the local building permit package.

Those tracks meet at the site plan. Freeze the house, driveway, well, septic, and shoreline improvements too early and you can discover a wetland, floodplain, or setback conflict after money is spent on design. Start with a survey, township ordinance check, flood-zone lookup, and wetland screening before you treat the footprint as final.

Michigan EGLE Permits That Commonly Apply

EGLE’s Water Resources Division regulates many activities where land meets water. For most shoreline, wetland, dock, dredge, fill, or Great Lakes bottomland projects, applications go through the EGLE/USACE Joint Permit Application in MiEnviro so state and federal reviews can run together.

Part 301 — Inland Lakes and Streams

Part 301 covers inland lakes and streams (not the Great Lakes themselves). EGLE requires a permit for activities that include dredging or filling bottomland; constructing or placing a structure on bottomland; constructing, reconfiguring, or expanding a marina; interfering with natural flow; or creating/connecting certain artificial waterways within 500 feet of an inland lake or stream. Practical examples EGLE calls out include permanent docks or boat hoists left in year-round, riprap and seawalls, dredging waterward of the ordinary high-water mark, and beach sanding below the water line. Seasonal private docks/hoists can be exempt when they meet EGLE’s stated conditions.

Part 303 — Wetlands Protection

Under Part 303, you generally need an EGLE permit before placing fill, dredging/removing soil, constructing a use or development, or draining surface water in a regulated wetland. EGLE’s wetlands FAQ explains that wetlands are regulated when they are connected to or within 1,000 feet of a Great Lake or Lake St. Clair, connected to or within 500 feet of an inland lake, pond, river, or stream, more than 5 acres even if isolated, or otherwise identified under the statute. Inventory maps are only a starting point—EGLE stresses that an on-site investigation is required to identify wetlands on a property.

Part 325 — Great Lakes Submerged Lands (Grand Traverse Bay)

Grand Traverse Bay is Lake Michigan water. Part 325 requires an EGLE permit for construction on Great Lakes bottomlands below the statutory ordinary high-water mark—including filling, dredging, and structures such as docks, boat lifts, or seawalls. EGLE states that projects on the Great Lakes that require an EGLE permit also require a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers permit. EGLE publishes the Lake Michigan/Huron statutory OHWM as 580.5 feet IGLD 1985 (579.8 feet on IGLD 1955) on its Ordinary High Water Mark page. EGLE’s shore-protection FAQ adds that if the water level is at or above the proposed structure, a permit is required regardless of the OHWM elevation.

Part 91 — Soil Erosion and Sedimentation Control

Part 91 generally requires a permit for earth changes that disturb one or more acres or that are within 500 feet of a lake or stream. In Grand Traverse County, Soil Erosion & Sedimentation Control is the designated County Enforcing Agency; the county’s Part 91 ordinance designates the Grand Traverse County Health Department to administer and enforce the program. Nearly every lakefront excavation—driveway cuts, foundation digs, septic fields, shoreline grading—falls inside that 500-foot trigger. Get the SESC plan and permit lined up before earthwork, not after the excavator arrives.

Township Zoning: Setbacks, Greenbelts, and Vegetation

EGLE does not replace township zoning. Waterfront setbacks, shared-access (“keyhole”) rules, tree-cutting strips, and natural-vegetation buffers are written into local ordinances and vary by township and zoning district. Confirm the parcel’s zoning map and the current ordinance text with the township before design. Examples you can verify in published ordinances:

Elmwood Township (Leelanau County side of West Bay) and other neighboring communities have their own shoreland rules. If your lot is outside Grand Traverse County, read that township’s ordinance rather than assuming the numbers above apply. When ordinances conflict with EGLE permits, you typically need both approvals—the stricter condition controls the design.

High Water and Shoreline Erosion on the Great Lakes

Lake Michigan water levels rise and fall over multi-year cycles. During high-water periods, wave run-up and bluff or beach erosion can threaten structures that previously felt safe. EGLE’s shore-protection materials emphasize that poorly designed hard armor can increase damage to neighboring properties and disrupt natural shoreline processes, and that new shoreline hardening should be approached carefully. Permanent seawalls, riprap, and similar protection on Great Lakes bottomlands need Part 325 (and USACE) authorization; inland-lake shore protection typically falls under Part 301. Work with a contractor who understands EGLE preferences for natural shoreline treatments where they can address erosion, and do not assume emergency rock can be placed first and permitted later.

FEMA Flood Zones and Local Floodplain Rules

Before you buy or design, look up the parcel on FEMA’s flood maps and on Grand Traverse County’s Flood Zone Dashboard. Special Flood Hazard Areas (for example Zones A or AE) are high-risk 1%-annual-chance floodplains; coastal mapping can also show high-velocity wave areas (VE). Michigan’s floodplain management guidance explains that buildings in Special Flood Hazard Areas must have the lowest floor elevated to at least the Base Flood Elevation plus 1 foot under the Michigan Construction Code. Flood insurance, foundation type, and finished-floor elevation are design drivers—not afterthoughts. Your township or city building official is the local gatekeeper for floodplain development permits alongside the building permit.

Wells, Septic, and Isolation Near Water

Most rural waterfront lots use a private well and on-site septic system permitted by the local health department. That process—site evaluation, isolation distances, system type, and sequencing with the building permit—is covered in detail in our Well and Septic Guide for Building Near Traverse City. Do not treat this section as a substitute for that guide.

Two waterfront-specific points from Grand Traverse County materials:

  • County officials have described the Grand Traverse County sanitary code as requiring 50 feet of isolation from surface waters and from wells (with a stated vertical separation to groundwater)—constraints that shrink usable drainfield area on narrow lake lots.
  • Grand Traverse County’s property-transfer evaluation regulation for onsite water and sewage systems applies when a dwelling is located within 300 feet of surface water, measured from the closest part of the dwelling to the surface water or high-water mark if available.

Place the house, well, septic, replacement area, and shoreline setbacks on one scaled site plan early. Browse well drilling and excavating contractors when you are ready for field work.

Seawalls, Riprap, Docks, and Marine Work

Shoreline structures are separate from the house permit path:

  • Permanent docks, boat lifts, seawalls, riprap, dredging, and fill on inland lakes/streams → evaluate Part 301 (and wetlands under Part 303 if present).
  • Same activities on Grand Traverse Bay bottomlands → Part 325 plus USACE, via the Joint Permit Application.
  • Township rules may separately limit dock width/number, shared waterfront use, side-yard storage of hoists, and shoreline vegetation—even when EGLE issues a permit.

EGLE’s inland lakes program notes that vertical walls can have negative effects and recommends natural shoreline treatments when they can protect property from erosion. Hire experienced seawall, retaining wall, and excavating contractors, and keep EGLE authorization in hand before equipment enters the water.

How to Sequence Permits With Your Builder

Use this order as a planning framework; your township or EGLE district staff may adjust it for the parcel:

  1. Due diligence before closing or final design. Boundary/OHWM survey, wetland screening, flood-zone check, township setback/greenbelt review, and a health-department site evaluation if the lot is unsewered.
  2. Concept site plan. House, garage, driveway, well, septic, replacement area, and any dock/shore protection drawn to scale against setbacks and isolation distances.
  3. EGLE / USACE Joint Permit Application for wetlands or shoreline/bottomland work—submit before detailed drawings assume an unpermitted shoreline layout.
  4. Part 91 SESC permit through Grand Traverse County for earth changes within 500 feet of water (or ≥1 acre).
  5. Well and septic permits through the health department (see the well and septic guide).
  6. Township land-use / zoning and building permits—the track covered in our Grand Traverse County building permits guide. Many building departments will not release permits until septic/well status and SESC are addressed.
  7. Construction staking and inspections. Keep EGLE, SESC, health department, and building inspections on the same calendar as your builder’s schedule.

For seasonal timing—frost, spring thaw, and busy summer shoreline crews—see when to start building in Northern Michigan. For builder selection on complex lake lots, see how to choose a home builder in Traverse City and the Traverse City home builders hub.

Practical Waterfront Due-Diligence Checklist

  • ☐ Confirm the parcel’s township, zoning district, and current waterfront setback/vegetation rules in the ordinance PDF—not a listing flyer.
  • ☐ Pull FEMA / Grand Traverse County flood-zone information for the exact address or PIN.
  • ☐ Walk the lot for wetland indicators; budget for a professional delineation if maps or soils suggest wetlands.
  • ☐ Identify whether shoreline work is on an inland lake/stream (Part 301) or Grand Traverse Bay (Part 325) and whether USACE review applies.
  • ☐ Ask Grand Traverse County SESC whether a Part 91 permit is required for your earth change (assume yes within 500 feet of water).
  • ☐ If unsewered, schedule the health-department site evaluation and sketch well/septic isolation against the house and shoreline.
  • ☐ Commission a survey that locates property lines and, where disputed, the ordinary high-water mark.
  • ☐ List every anticipated shoreline structure (dock, hoist, stairs, riprap, seawall) and which agency must approve it.
  • ☐ Choose a builder and excavator who have completed permitted waterfront projects in the same township.
  • ☐ Do not clear vegetation or start grading until SESC, township, and any EGLE authorizations that apply are in hand.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need an EGLE permit to build a house on a Grand Traverse waterfront lot?

A township or county building permit alone is not the full story. Work in regulated wetlands can require a Part 303 permit. Shoreline, dock, dredge, fill, or bottomland work on inland lakes and streams can require Part 301. Construction on Great Lakes bottomlands below the statutory ordinary high-water mark—including docks, boat lifts, and seawalls on Grand Traverse Bay—requires a Part 325 permit, and EGLE states those Great Lakes projects also need a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers permit. Many land/water-interface projects use the EGLE/USACE Joint Permit Application through MiEnviro. The house footprint itself may stay upland, but site grading, septic, shoreline work, and wetlands nearby often trigger state review.

Who administers Part 91 soil erosion permits in Grand Traverse County?

Grand Traverse County Soil Erosion and Sedimentation Control is the County Enforcing Agency for Part 91. County materials designate the Grand Traverse County Health Department as the agency that administers and enforces Part 91 locally. Statewide, Part 91 generally requires a permit for earth changes that disturb one or more acres or that are within 500 feet of a lake or stream—so nearly every waterfront home site needs local SESC review before excavation.

What waterfront setbacks apply around Traverse City?

Setbacks and vegetation rules are township-specific. Peninsula Township’s Schedule of Regulations lists a 60-foot setback from the ordinary high-water mark for the residential districts shown there, plus a 35-foot shoreline tree-cutting strip. Acme Township’s Waterfront Overlay applies within 50 feet of Grand Traverse Bay’s ordinary high-water mark (and named creeks/wetlands) and limits tree cutting in a 35-foot shore strip. East Bay Township defines riparian setback areas within 50 feet of most water bodies (larger along portions of the Boardman River) and requires vegetative buffers. Garfield Township requires a 50-foot setback from Silver and Boardman Lakes and a 35-foot riparian vegetative buffer. Long Lake Township waterside setbacks are commonly 50 feet, or 100 feet in the Natural Lakefront district. Always confirm the current ordinance for your parcel.

What is the ordinary high-water mark on Grand Traverse Bay?

For Part 325 Great Lakes Submerged Lands, EGLE publishes the statutory ordinary high-water mark for Lake Michigan/Huron as 580.5 feet International Great Lakes Datum 1985 (579.8 feet on the older IGLD 1955 datum). Activities such as dredging, filling, seawalls, docks, and other structures along and below that elevation require Part 325 permits. EGLE’s high-water shore-protection FAQ also notes that if the water level is at or above the proposed structure, a permit is required regardless of the OHWM elevation. Township ordinances may use the same concept for zoning setbacks—confirm how your township defines and measures it.

How should I sequence waterfront permits with my builder?

Start with due diligence: survey, wetland screening, flood-zone check, and township zoning setbacks before freezing the house footprint. Apply early for health-department well/septic approvals and the county Part 91 SESC permit. Submit any needed EGLE/USACE Joint Permit Application for wetlands or shoreline work before or in parallel with detailed design—not after excavation starts. Coordinate township land-use/zoning and building permits with the builder so EGLE, SESC, septic, and building reviews do not block each other. Hire waterfront-experienced excavators, marine/seawall contractors, and surveyors from the directory as the site plan firms up.
